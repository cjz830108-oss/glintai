ATS-friendly formatting tips

Many companies filter resumes with applicant tracking software before a human reads them. A few habits keep your resume readable to both:

  • Use standard headings: Summary, Experience, Education, Skills. Odd labels can confuse the parser.
  • Avoid images and text boxes: the software may skip content inside them, so keep text in the normal flow.
  • Use simple fonts: a clean sans-serif at a normal size reads better than decorative type.
  • Match keywords from the posting: if the job asks for 'inventory management', use that phrase where true, not a synonym.

Formatting should never replace substance. The AI draft gives you the structure; you supply the real achievements. A tidy, keyword-aligned resume gets past the filter, but the interviews come from what you actually did.

What goes in each resume section

A short cheatsheet keeps the draft honest. The summary is two or three lines of your strongest, most relevant trait, not a life story. Experience lists roles with bullets that start with active verbs and end with measurable results. Education names the degree, school, and date, with honors only if they matter to the role. Skills should match the posting, listing tools and methods you actually use. Keep each section tight; a hiring manager skims in seconds, so lead with proof, not padding.

Avoiding the AI robot voice

A common mistake is shipping the raw AI draft, which often reads like every other template: 'Results-driven professional with a passion for excellence.' That phrase tells a reader nothing and signals boilerplate. The fix is to make the voice yours.

Run the draft through the AI humanizer to break the uniformity, then rewrite any line that still feels hollow with a specific fact. Concrete beats clever every time. 'Managed social media' says little; 'grew the account from 400 to 2,000 followers in six months' says everything. After the humanizer softens the tone, swap vague wins for numbers, and let the robot voice disappear.

Tailoring to each job description

One resume rarely fits every role. After you write a resume with AI for the base version, tailoring each application takes minutes. Read the posting, note the three skills they repeat, and lead your experience with matches to those skills.

Reorder bullets so the most relevant achievement sits first. Use the company's language where it is accurate: if they say 'customer success', and your work was customer success, say that. If they want 'data analysis', surface the analysis you did. The AI cover letter resume guide walks through aligning a resume and letter to one posting so the story stays consistent. Turning duties into achievements

The single biggest resume upgrade is changing 'responsible for' lines into results. A duty says what you were supposed to do; an achievement says what changed because you did it. Start each bullet with an active verb, then attach a number.

Weak: Responsible for social media. Strong: Grew Instagram from 300 to 1,800 followers in five months by posting daily reels. Weak: Helped with inventory. Strong: Cut stockouts 30 percent by rebuilding the weekly order sheet. The AI draft often gives you the duty; your job is to add the number. The generator can suggest phrasing, but only you know the outcome, so fill that gap before you send. A resume that shows what changed is the one that earns the call.

Resume summary examples that work

The summary is the first thing read and the easiest to waste. A good one names your role, your top strength, and the result you deliver. For a retail lead: Retail supervisor with four years driving sales teams, known for cutting shrinkage by 18 percent. For a new grad: Recent logistics graduate who built a campus delivery app used by 600 students in its first term.

Notice neither uses 'passionate' or 'hardworking'. Those words are assumed; the reader wants proof of role and result. After the generator drafts a summary, run it through the humanizer and then swap any vague claim for a fact. A tight summary earns the skim that follows.

Building the skills section the right way

The skills list is where tailoring pays off most. Pull the exact terms from the posting and list the ones you genuinely have. If they want 'SQL' and 'reporting', and you have both, name them; do not write 'data stuff'. Group related skills so a scanner sees them fast: Technical, Tools, Languages.

Avoid padding the list with obvious basics like 'Microsoft Word' unless the role truly needs it. Every line should answer 'can this person do the job' with a yes. The grammar checker catches typos here too, and a misspelled skill name looks careless, so proof the section like any other.

Pairing your resume with a cover letter

A resume lists; a cover letter explains. Write the resume first, then the letter, so the two tell one story. The letter should open with the role and why you fit, then point to one achievement from the resume as proof. Keep it short: three short paragraphs beat a page of preamble.

The AI cover letter resume guide shows how to align both documents to a single posting so nothing contradicts. Mention the same metrics in both places, because a reader who sees '20 percent faster' on the resume and 'much faster' in the letter loses trust. Consistency is the quiet signal of a careful candidate.

Common resume mistakes to avoid

A few errors appear on nearly every weak resume. Lying about roles or numbers is the worst; it collapses the moment a reference checks. Typos rank high too, which is why the grammar checker pass is non-negotiable. Photo, columns, and graphics look nice but break ATS parsing, so skip them for the initial send. Finally, a one-size resume sent to fifty jobs reads as lazy; tailor at least the top third each time.

None of these are hard to fix, and the human-in-the-loop workflow handles most of them by design. Draft, humanize, proof, tailor, send. That order turns a blank page into a real application in well under an hour, and it keeps you honest at every step.

How long should a resume be

For most people, one page is the right length, especially with under ten years of experience. Two pages are fine for senior roles with deep history, but never pad to fill space. A hiring manager prefers a dense, relevant single page over a thin two-page spread. Cut older, irrelevant jobs rather than stretching them, and let the generator's structure keep the layout tight. Quality of bullets beats quantity of lines every time.

Using AI without losing your voice

The fear with any AI tool is that the output replaces you. It should not. Treat the draft as a rough cut from a fast assistant, not a final answer. Read it aloud; if a line would never leave your mouth, change it. Add the details only you know, the numbers only you measured, the turns of phrase that are yours. The humanizer can soften tone, but the substance that makes the resume yours has to come from you. Used this way, AI saves time without erasing identity.

Action verbs that strengthen bullets

The verb at the start of each bullet sets the tone. Swap weak openers like 'did' or 'was' for specific ones: led, built, cut, launched, improved, negotiated, trained, designed, resolved, delivered. Match the verb to the result: use 'cut' or 'reduced' for savings, 'launched' for new work, 'trained' for people. A list of strong verbs from the generator gives you options, but pick the one that is true. Precise verbs make a short bullet carry weight, which is how a one-page resume stays powerful.

What to leave off your resume

Some items hurt more than they help. Skip your full home address beyond city, your high school if you finished college, and hobbies unrelated to the role. Leave off reasons for leaving past jobs; save that for the interview. Do not list references as 'available on request' unless asked, since it wastes space. Keeping these off leaves room for achievements, which is what gets interviews. The grammar checker still applies to whatever remains, so proof the final cut before sending.
