Fiction is creative, not formulaic — but the craft still has chores: tightening prose, varying rhythm, catching errors. Why privacy matters for fiction

Your drafts are original IP. Pasting chapters into a tool that keeps them is a risk no writer should take lightly. A browser-based, no-signup tool processes text on the page, so unpublished work stays on your device. No account means no cloud copy, no training on your plot twists, no editor who can read chapter twelve before you've finished chapter three. Treat any tool that asks you to log in before pasting a sentence as a tool that now owns a copy of it. Free, private, and instant beats free, convenient, and forever-scanned.

The novelist's pipeline, and where AI belongs

A manuscript passes through four distinct stages, and each needs a different kind of help. Drafting comes first — the fast, ugly, get-it-down phase. Anne Lamott gave it the most useful permission slip in *Bird by Bird* (1994): the "shitty first drafts" rule. The first version exists only so later versions have something to fix; polishing while drafting is doing two jobs at once and finishing neither. Draft fast, revise slow.

Then comes the developmental edit, which works on story: are the stakes rising, does the midpoint change anything, is the ending earned. The line edit works on prose — rhythm, repetition, whether every scene earns its place. Last is the proofread, a mechanical sweep for typos on what is essentially a finished book. Writers stall when they mix stages, running proofread-level polish on chapter one while act two has no ending. Knowing which stage you are in tells you which tool to open.

AI genuinely helps at three points. Brainstorming: a list of ten reasons your detective would lie to her partner costs nothing, and you only need one. Consistency checks: flagging that a character's eye color changed between chapter three and chapter nineteen. Gap-spotting: a summary of your own manuscript shows the subplot you abandoned in act two. Where AI hurts is voice and originality: wholesale model prose trends toward the median — grammatical, smooth, strangely characterless. Beats and tropes are formulaic, so AI can assist; voice and theme are the differentiators, and AI flattens both. This is the wrong place to save time.

One prompting pattern earns its keep: the adversarial beta reader. Paste a scene and instruct the tool to attack it — "List every reason a reader would stop reading this scene. Find where the motivation contradicts itself." You stay the writer; the tool plays the hostile reviewer who tells you the truth your friends won't.

Improve the prose

Good fiction is built on sentences the reader forgets they are reading. The tools below help you hear your own prose again after you have stared at it too long.

Grammar checker

How to use it: paste a scene or a chapter and let the Grammar Checker flag the typos your eye skips on the tenth read. It is built for long manuscripts, where the brain autocorrects your own mistakes.

Worked example — a paragraph before and after:

  • Before: "The captain steped onto the deck, and he was worried by the storm that was gathering on the horizon."
  • After: "The captain stepped onto the deck, worried by the storm gathering on the horizon."

Notice the second version drops a weak "was worried" and a repeated "the." The checker catches the missing "e" in "steped" and the doubled "the"; you decide whether to cut the passive habit.

Most useful on a full chapter. Run it once the draft is cold, a day or two after writing.

Paraphraser

How to use it: highlight one awkward sentence and let the Paraphraser offer a rewording that keeps the scene intact. Use it to test an alternative rhythm, not to replace your wording wholesale.

Quick list of when it earns its place:

  • A sentence reads true but clunks on the tongue.
  • Two characters sound identical and you want to split their voices.
  • You have repeated a structure three times in one page and need a fourth shape.

Paste only the sentence. Paraphrasing a whole chapter bleeds the voice out of it.

AI humanizer

How to use it: when you draft with an assistant, run the flat passages through the AI Humanizer so co-written lines match your narrative voice. AI-assisted text tends to smear into the same polite, weightless tone; the humanizer roughens it back toward something with a heartbeat.

A short scenario: a model sketched your tavern description — correct but smooth to the point of glass. Humanize it, then read aloud. If it still sounds like a brochure, cut half the adjectives and let one concrete detail do the work.

Readability analyzer

How to use it: check that dialogue and exposition read at the level your audience expects with the Readability Analyzer. It scores sentence length and word difficulty so you can see where a page went heavy.

Watch for sudden shifts. A fight scene at grade-nine reading level followed by a council scene at grade-fifteen tells the reader, without words, that one matters more. Smooth the gap on purpose, not by accident.

Line editing is not developmental editing

Writers use "editing" for two different jobs, and confusing them wastes months. A developmental edit returns a revision letter — on a 90,000-word novel, professional editors commonly deliver five to ten single-spaced pages of argument plus margin comments, after three to six weeks. It fights about structure, stakes, and motivation. A line edit never mentions your plot; it works sentence by sentence on rhythm, repetition, and whether dialogue sounds like the character who speaks it.

AI can genuinely assist with the line-level work: catching the word you used nine times, the three sentences in a row with the same shape. It cannot hold a whole novel in its head the way a developmental editor does. And if you hire one professional, choose by diagnosis: beautiful sentences in the wrong scene are still in the wrong scene — fix structure first.

Manage the work

Revision is easier when you can see the shape of the whole book, not just the page in front of you.

AI text summarizer

How to use it: summarize a chapter you wrote weeks ago with the AI Text Summarizer to rebuild context before you edit. It also tracks plot threads if you feed it your scene notes.

Try this: paste every scene card for a subplot and ask for a one-paragraph summary. You will see at a glance where a thread was dropped in act two and never picked up. The same trick works on a single dense chapter: summarize, then check against what you meant to say.

Word counter

How to use it: track chapter and section lengths with the Word Counter to keep pacing intentional. A mystery that runs 4,000 words per chapter then suddenly 9,000 signals a sag the reader will feel before they name it.

A simple habit: log the count at the end of each writing day. Patterns emerge — chapters run long on dialogue and short on setting, or act three always balloons. Genre norms give a rough target: category romance runs 50,000–55,000 words, most thrillers 70,000–90,000, and adult fantasy often stretches past 100,000 — debuts usually need to stay under about 120,000, because publishers price doorstopper first novels badly.

Series bibles and continuity timelines

Long fiction breaks without a memory system. Robert Jordan's Wheel of Time ran fourteen full-length novels, and after his death his personal notes became the backbone of *The Wheel of Time Companion* (Tor, 2014) — hundreds of pages of character and timeline entries built from Jordan's obsessive records. A series bible is the same idea at kitchen-table scale: character sheets (appearance, speech habits, what each person would never do), an in-story timeline, and a running list of facts you have already committed to.

AI tools help you maintain it without loving it. Summarize each finished chapter into a three-sentence entry and paste it into your bible the day you finish; six months later it answers in seconds instead of an afternoon of rereading. Bigger gaps — actual plot holes — are their own repair job; see fixing plot holes with AI.

The one-pass-per-concern revision method

Revision stalls when you fix everything at once: every sentence triggers four decisions and you make none of them. Professional editors work in passes, one concern per pass, in this order:

  • Structure pass: read the outline, not the prose. Does every scene change something? Shawn Coyne's Story Grid Five Commandments — inciting incident, progressive complication, crisis, climax, resolution — is a serviceable per-scene test; skip it for deliberately quiet connective tissue.
  • Character-motivation pass: for each scene, state what the viewpoint character wants and what they do when they don't get it. Dwight Swain's Scene/Sequel pattern (goal, conflict, disaster, then reaction, dilemma, decision) works as a checklist here, not a law — literary fiction breaks it on purpose.
  • Dialogue pass: read the dialogue aloud, alone. Fix voices that sound identical and speeches where a character explains things both already know.
  • Prose pass: now the grammar checker, readability analyzer, and paraphraser earn their keep, on a manuscript that is already structurally finished.

Run the passes in that order and you will never polish a scene you later cut.

A quick scenario

Maya is 60,000 words into a fantasy novel. She returns after a week away and cannot remember why the spy switched sides. She pastes chapters four through nine into the AI Text Summarizer; the summary shows the turn happened off-page, in a scene she outlined but never wrote. She opens the Grammar Checker on chapter seven, where "their" should be "they're" and a sentence repeats "quietly" three times. She highlights the clunkiest line for the Paraphraser, softens two AI-drafted descriptions with the AI Humanizer, and runs the Readability Analyzer on the climax to confirm it stays tense. Her Word Counter tells her act two is 3,000 words heavier than act one — so she trims. Nothing touched a server. The manuscript never left her laptop.

Common mistakes to avoid

Writers new to these tools trip the same few ways. Skip them.

  • Running the humanizer on prose you wrote yourself. It is for AI-assisted drafts; on your own lines it adds noise.
  • Paraphrasing whole chapters. Voice lives in the small choices; bulk paraphrasing averages them out.
  • Treating a green grammar check as "done." It misses weak rhythm, flat imagery, and a character who suddenly talks like someone else.
  • Chasing a lower readability score. Shorter is not always better; a calm page can carry long sentences on purpose.
  • Editing prose before structure is settled — months of line polish on chapters the story no longer needs.
  • Forgetting the tools are for revision. Draft first. Polish after. The spark is yours.

Free vs paid

Free here covers revision and checking. What the paid market adds, honestly: Scrivener ($59 one-time) remains the standard drafting app for novelists; Plottr and Novelcrafter handle outlining and series bibles; Sudowrite is the best-known AI drafting subscription; AutoCrit and ProWritingAid sell deep style reports. Those are workflow purchases. If your problem is the second pass, the cold reread, or the word you used nine times, the free set already does the job:

  • Catch typos in a long chapter — Grammar Checker vs a full editor suite.
  • Reword one clumsy line — Paraphraser vs a paid rewrite assistant.
  • Smooth AI-assisted drafts — AI Humanizer vs a humanize subscription.
  • Check reading level — Readability Analyzer vs a writing-coach app.
  • Rebuild context on old chapters — AI Text Summarizer vs story-bible software.
  • Track chapter lengths — Word Counter vs manuscript trackers.

Two industry realities worth knowing. Publish through Amazon's Kindle Unlimited and KDP requires exclusivity — the enrolled book cannot be sold anywhere else — and since September 2023 KDP asks at publish time whether content is AI-generated. Short-fiction markets learned what undifferentiated machine prose does: Clarkesworld closed submissions in February 2023 after more than 500 machine-generated stories flooded in within a single month, by the magazine's own count. Editors screen harder now; a manuscript with a real human voice has never been more valuable. The legal backdrop is unsettled — the Authors Guild sued OpenAI in September 2023 over training on copyrighted books — but unpublished manuscripts are the one thing you control completely, which is why no-upload tools are not a gimmick.

A healthy writing habit

Draft freely first; the tools are for revision, not the spark. Grammar-check the finished chapter, humanize any flat passage, and summarize earlier chapters when you return to the manuscript. Used this way, they protect your time, not replace your imagination. Keep the privacy rule in front of you: no signup, no upload, your words on your machine. That is the whole deal.
