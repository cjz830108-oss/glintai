How to Write Product Descriptions with AI Without Sounding Robotic

Updated 2026-10-04 · 11 min read · by Glint AI Editorial Team

AI can draft product descriptions in seconds, then ruin your brand by sounding like every other store. The trick is to use AI for the first 80 percent and you for the last 20 — the voice, the specifics, the reason a real person should care. Done free and in your browser, it scales your catalog without flattening it. This guide shows the FAB workflow, the edits that matter, the SEO reality of manufacturer boilerplate, and the one legal line you never let AI cross.

Key takeaways

  • Use AI for the first 80 percent and your voice for the last 20 percent.
  • Feed specific inputs and a banned-words list so the draft is not generic.
  • Always humanize the raw output or it reads as synthetic to customers and detectors.
  • Add one true, specific line per product that AI could not invent.
  • Browser-based writing never uploads unreleased specs or pricing.

What AI-assisted description writing does

It takes a few facts — product, material, use, audience — and produces a draft with structure and benefits. You then edit for voice and accuracy. The tool handles the blank page; you handle the judgment. That split is what keeps the output useful instead of generic, and it is far faster than writing fifty listings from scratch.

The discipline behind a good description has a name in copywriting: FAB — Feature → Advantage → Benefit. Customers do not buy features; they buy what features do for them, and the chain is how you get there without guesswork.

The FAB chain applied to real product lines

Take a 12 oz ceramic mug:

  • Feature: double-fired glaze.
  • Advantage: resists chips and dishwasher fading.
  • Benefit: it is still the good mug a year from now, not the chipped one at the back of the cabinet.

Now a 17.5-micron merino base layer:

  • Feature: fine-gauge merino wool (17.5 micron, finer than the common 19.5).
  • Advantage: softer next to skin and naturally odor-resistant.
  • Benefit: four days on the trail without a wash and nobody notices.

The rule that makes FAB useful: every feature sentence must terminate in a benefit sentence. If your chain stops at the advantage, you have written a spec sheet. If it never touches the feature, you have written an ad that cannot be verified.

Why browser-only matters

Product copy often includes unreleased specs, supplier names, and pricing you would not paste into a random site. A browser-based writer processes the text on the page and never uploads it. For confidential catalogs, local generation is the safe default, and it also keeps your competitive details off someone else's training set.

This is not paranoia. Pre-announcement product lines are competitive intelligence, and supplier agreements frequently include confidentiality terms that cover spec sheets. A paste into an arbitrary web tool can breach them without anyone noticing until the catalog leaks.

When to use it

  • Large catalogs where writing each listing by hand is impractical.
  • New variants that differ only slightly from an existing item.
  • Non-native copy you want polished to native fluency.
  • A/B draft sets to test which angle converts.
  • SEO scaffolding where structure matters as much as prose.
  • Seasonal refreshes where old copy needs a fast rewrite.

How to write well with AI

Feed specific inputs, not "write a description." Give the audience, the one benefit that matters, and a forbidden-words list (no "best," no "amazing"). Then humanize the draft so it reads like a person. Add one true, specific line per product that AI could not invent. Structure that converts

A description that sells has a shape, and it is built for scanning:

  • An opening line that names the product and the payoff in one breath — no "Introducing," no "Say hello to."
  • Two or three sentences of sensory, specific prose. "The 3 mm leather pulls tight with a sound you can hear" beats "premium quality leather construction."
  • Spec bullets for the scanners who arrived knowing what they want: material, dimensions, weight, care, compatibility.
  • Size and dimension clarity in real units. Ambiguous sizing is one of the top drivers of apparel returns; flat measurements (pit to pit, length in inches) cost two lines and cut the "smaller than expected" return reason at the source.

The SEO reality: unique copy or invisible copy

Here is the part most store owners learn too late: manufacturers ship ready-made product copy, and hundreds of shops paste it. Search engines consolidate near-duplicate text and typically show one version — usually the manufacturer's own site or the largest retailer, not the newest shop. Original writing is not a branding preference; it is the difference between existing in search results and not.

Originality has a second, cheaper win: include the terms buyers actually type. Model numbers, SKU names, and use-case phrases — "8-inch non-stick omelette pan," "SN95 brake pads" — are how people search when they know what they want. Boilerplate almost never contains them; your copy should.

Length by category

  • Commodity items — socks, cables, phone cases: 50–100 words plus spec bullets. Nobody reads a paragraph about a USB cable; they read the spec list.
  • Considered purchases — mattresses, espresso machines, office chairs: 200–400 words. The buyer has questions and objections; answer the top three in prose and let bullets carry the rest.
  • Either way, mobile first. Most store traffic is on phones, which means the opening line and the first three bullets carry most of the decision. Put the payoff up front; the long tail of detail can live below the fold.

A step-by-step method

  1. Gather facts — material, size, use, audience, differentiator, model number.
  2. Prompt with constraints — audience, the one benefit that matters, banned filler words.
  3. Generate the draft and skim for generic praise.
  4. Fact-check every claim against the spec sheet — this is the legal step, not the style step.
  5. Humanize so it reads like a person wrote it.
  6. Add one specific line only you could write, then publish.

The rule that keeps you out of court: AI never invents specs

An AI model does not know whether your jacket is waterproof to 10,000 mm or whether your supplement carries a third-party certification. It will write plausible-sounding credentials anyway, because plausible text is what it generates. In the US that path runs straight into the FTC's truth-in-advertising rules: claims must be substantiated, and a certification you do not hold is false advertising — "FDA-approved," "CE certified," "organic," and "medical grade" are all claims people have been fined over.

The workflow fix is mechanical. Feed the model the spec sheet in the prompt, forbid it from adding anything absent from the input, and treat the human fact-check as a gate, not a suggestion. Style edits can be rushed. Claim edits cannot.

A worked example

  • Raw facts: "ceramic mug, 12 oz, double-fired glaze, dishwasher safe, gifts for designers"
  • AI draft: a generic benefit paragraph ("elevate your morning ritual...") — grammatical, pleasant, interchangeable with every mug on the internet
  • Humanized: "A 12 oz ceramic mug that survives the dishwasher and looks at home on a designer's desk."

The humanized line keeps the fact but drops the filler, which is the whole point. Run the same product through FAB and you get the three-sentence version from the top of this guide — feature, advantage, benefit — which is a complete description for a commodity item.

Draft vs finished compared

  • Raw AI draft: robotic, generic praise, reads like a template, converts poorly.
  • Humanized draft: natural voice, reads like a person, converts better.
  • Plus a specific true line: distinctly yours, converts best — and is the only version a competitor cannot generate in ten seconds.

Power words vs hype

Power words earn their place when they are anchored: "snag-free," "cold-brew ready," "fits in a jacket pocket." Hype is the same instinct with nothing underneath: "amazing," "best-in-class," "revolutionary." The test is falsifiability — a claim a skeptical customer could check. "Warm to 14°F" is checkable and therefore believable. "The warmest jacket you'll ever own" is unverifiable, and unverifiable superlatives train returning customers to quietly discount everything else you say.

Where social proof goes

Reviews and quotes belong at the decision point: after the benefit prose, adjacent to the add-to-cart action — not as a star icon floating at the top of the page. One verified-buyer sentence that mentions a specific detail ("the zipper survived a season of daily use") outperforms a row of anonymous stars, because it repeats the proof structure the description itself is built on.

A quick scenario: a solo ecommerce seller

A solo seller lists fifty products and stalls at description ten. Using a free AI writer, they feed each item's specs and get a structured draft in seconds, then spend thirty seconds adding one specific line per product.

The catalog fills in an afternoon instead of a month. The descriptions read consistently because the human edit enforces voice. No account, no upload of supplier pricing to a third party. When the seller notices returns clustering on one sweater — "smaller than expected" in the return reasons — they add flat measurements to every apparel listing. Returns on that item drop, and two lines of spec clarity have now paid for the entire afternoon.

The habit compounds. Every new SKU ships with a real description, the store looks maintained, and conversion creeps up because the copy finally says something. The AI did the typing; the seller did the brand, and customers can tell the difference.

Common mistakes

  • Publishing the raw draft — "best," "amazing," "top-quality" appear by default and erase your voice.
  • Feeding too little context — vague in, vague out.
  • Letting AI invent specs or certifications — the fastest route to a false-advertising complaint and a refund dispute you cannot win.
  • Skipping measurements — ambiguous sizing drives returns you pay for twice, in shipping and in trust.
  • Duplicating the manufacturer's copy — invisible in search and interchangeable with every competitor selling the same item.
  • Pasting unreleased specs into a web tool — leaks data you wanted private.

Who should use it (and who shouldn't)

Use AI to draft descriptions for any catalog where volume beats hand-crafting. Skip it for hero products where every word is a brand moment — write those yourself. The tool scales the many; you own the few that matter most.
