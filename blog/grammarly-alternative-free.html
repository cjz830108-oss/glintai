Why Look for a Free Grammarly Alternative?

Grammarly built its name on a free tier, but that free tier is thin. A grammarly alternative free of the account wall and the upsell still delivers the grammar fixes you write for. You get basic spelling and a few grammar fixes. Advanced corrections, full-sentence rewrites, tone detection, and plagiarism checks sit behind the Premium plan. At roughly twelve dollars per month billed annually, that adds up to about one hundred forty-four dollars a year for a single user.

For students, freelancers, and small teams, that price stings. Worse, the free plan nudges you toward upgrading on nearly every document. Many users open Grammarly, see a red underline, click it, and land on an upgrade prompt instead of a fix. That friction is exactly why people type grammarly alternative free into search engines every month.

A genuine free alternative should do more than mimic the free tier. It should catch real errors, respect your privacy, and ideally ask for no account at all. The tools below clear that bar. Several run entirely in your browser, so your drafts never leave your machine. That matters when you are editing client copy, interview notes, or unpublished fiction.

The best part is choice. You can pair a no-signup checker for quick scans with a deeper tool for long manuscripts. None of the picks in this list require a credit card, and most never ask for your email.

Free does not mean weak. The checkers in this list catch the errors that embarrass you in front of a client or a professor. You lose some polish and a few advanced reports, but you keep your money and your privacy. That trade is worth it for most daily writing, and it is the reason this list exists.

How We Tested and Ranked

We did not rank these tools by vibes. Each candidate passed four tests. Is the core feature free? Can you use it without creating an account? How accurate is the grammar engine on a mixed sample of casual and formal text? And how many languages does it support?

Our test set included forty sentences with common errors: subject-verb disagreement, comma splices, wrong homophones, tense shifts, and awkward passive voice. We ran the same set through every tool and noted false positives and missed errors. Tools that flagged correct text as wrong lost points. Tools that stayed quiet on real mistakes lost more.

Privacy weighed heavily. A grammar checker reads everything you paste. If that text ships to a server you do not control, your ideas, client names, and half-finished pitches become someone else's training data. We gave top marks to checkers that process text on your device or in your browser session without storing it.

Finally, we checked the signup wall. A tool that demands an email before you can paste a paragraph is not really free in spirit. The fewer the fields, the higher the rank.

We also weighted real-world speed. A checker that takes ten seconds to return results will not get used, no matter how accurate. We preferred tools that respond quickly on a normal laptop and do not throttle free users into oblivion after two checks. A fast free tool beats a slow perfect one because you will actually open it.

10 Best Free Grammarly Alternatives

LanguageTool

LanguageTool is the closest direct rival to Grammarly. The free plan checks grammar and style in more than thirty languages, which makes it the top pick for multilingual writers. You get around ten thousand characters per check on the free tier, enough for most emails and short articles. It runs as a browser extension, a desktop app, and a web editor. Privacy is solid because you can self-host the server if you want full control. The downside is that advanced style suggestions require a paid plan.

QuillBot

QuillBot started as a paraphraser but now ships a free grammar checker inside its web app. The checker is accurate on sentence-level fixes and pairs well with its rewriting tools. The free plan limits you to one hundred twenty-five words per paraphrase, but the grammar scanner itself is open for longer text. It is a strong secondary tool when you want to fix and rewrite in one place. See our free paraphraser if rewriting is your main goal.

Hemingway Editor

Hemingway takes a different angle. Instead of flagging every comma, it scores your writing for readability. It highlights long sentences, passive voice, and weak adverbs in color. The web version is free to use, though the desktop app costs money. Writers who overwrite will love the blunt feedback. It will not catch every grammatical slip, so pair it with a true checker for final polish.

ProWritingAid

ProWritingAid is a favorite among novelists because it reports on repeated words, pacing, and structure. The free plan lets you edit documents up to five hundred words at a time in the web editor. It integrates with Scrivener and Google Docs. The depth of its reports is unmatched, but the word cap and the busy interface can slow you down on longer work.

Wordtune

Wordtune focuses on rewriting rather than pure correction. You select a sentence and it offers clearer, shorter, or more formal versions. The free plan grants a limited number of monthly rewrites. It is excellent for non-native speakers who want to sound natural. Grammar checking is lighter than dedicated tools, so treat it as a complement rather than a complete solution.

DeepL Write

DeepL Write combines translation heritage with grammar and style suggestions. It supports English and German prominently, with more languages rolling out over time. The suggestions feel human because DeepL trained on high-quality translations. The free version is generous for casual use. If you already use DeepL for translation, Write slots in naturally without a second account.

Sapling

Sapling targets business communication. Its free tier checks grammar in your browser as you type in web apps. It is lighter than Grammarly but faster to load on modest hardware. Sales and support teams use it to keep messages clean. The free plan covers personal use well, while team features sit behind paid tiers.

Google Docs

Do not overlook the tool you already have. Google Docs ships a free grammar and spell checker that improves with each update. It underlines errors in blue and offers one-click fixes. For collaborative documents it is hard to beat. The suggestions are basic compared to AI tools, but the price, free, is right and no extra signup is needed if you use Gmail.

Glint AI Grammar Checker

Glint AI Grammar Checker is our pick for the no-signup, privacy-first writer. It runs in your browser, needs no account, and processes your text without sending drafts to a server that stores them. You paste, get corrections, and leave. There is no paywall on the core check and no email form blocking the door. For sensitive client work, that client-side design is the real advantage. Open the Glint AI Grammar Checker to try it in seconds.

ChatGPT and Claude

Modern chatbots can proofread surprisingly well. Paste a paragraph and ask for grammar and clarity fixes. The catch is privacy: your text becomes part of the model session, and free tiers may use it for training unless you disable that setting. Use them for brainstorming and heavy rewrites, not for confidential drafts. They are powerful but not built as quiet background checkers for private work.

How to Switch Without Losing Your Writing Flow

Changing tools should not break your habit. First, install a browser-extension checker so corrections appear wherever you type. Second, keep one web tool bookmarked for deep edits, such as the Glint AI Grammar Checker. Third, build a two-step routine: write freely, then run a check before you publish.

Export your old Grammarly documents as plain text and re-run them through your new tool to catch anything missed. Most alternatives import standard formats without trouble. Give the new routine two weeks; by then the red underlines will feel familiar again and the upgrade prompts will be a distant memory.

If you want a step-by-step method, our grammar checker guide walks through the process. The goal is momentum: a lighter tool you actually open beats a heavier one you avoid because of the login screen.

One more tip keeps the switch painless: keep a shared note of your favorite settings. If a tool lets you pick British or American English, set it once and screenshot it. Switching tools should feel like changing pens, not changing operating systems. Small comforts are what make a new habit stick past the first week.
