Why privacy matters more here

If English is not your first language, you may paste personal essays, job applications, or client work into a tool. A browser-based, no-signup tool keeps that text on your device. You get help without handing your words to a server you cannot see.

For non-native writers this matters twice over. First, you are more likely to paste drafts you feel self-conscious about — half-finished sentences, guesses at idiom. Second, those drafts are often sensitive: a cover letter with your name and address, a visa statement, a message to a professor. Privacy is not a setting you can switch off later; it has to be true from the first keystroke. With Glint AI, the grammar and style checks run in your browser, so your words are never uploaded and never stored — no account, no trace. When your draft never leaves the device, you can take risks — a bolder opening line, a half-learned idiom — and learn faster because nothing is watching.

The detector problem you should know about

A documented issue you should know about. Stanford research published in 2023 (Liang and colleagues, in the journal Patterns) tested several GPT detectors and found they misclassified more than 60% of TOEFL essays by non-native speakers as AI-generated — far above the rate for native-speaker essays. The likely cause: detectors reward unpredictability, so careful, correct, formulaic prose reads as "machine-like." Writing well by the rules gets punished for looking too much like the rules.

Institutions noticed: Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in August 2023, citing accuracy concerns, and Turnitin's own guidance says a score should never be proof on its own. The takeaway: never treat a score as a verdict — if you are flagged unfairly, this documented bias is the first fact to cite.

Get the mechanics right

Mechanics are the floor, not the ceiling: get them right and readers stop noticing the language and start noticing your ideas.

Grammar Checker

Catch the errors native speakers spot instantly — articles, prepositions, subject-verb agreement — that are easy to miss in a second language.

How to use it: Paste a paragraph into the Grammar Checker and read the suggestions one at a time, not all at once. For each fix, ask why — "why is it 'a' here and 'the' there?" — so the rule sticks.

Worked example — common fixes it catches:

  • "I went to the home" → "I went home" (no article with home as destination)
  • "She is married with a teacher" → "She is married to a teacher" (preposition)
  • "The news was very important for we" → "...important for us" (object pronoun)
  • "He don't like it" → "He doesn't like it" (subject-verb agreement)

Word Counter

Hit exact limits on essays and applications, where a few words over can cost you.

How to use it: Paste your draft into the Word Counter before you submit. It shows total words, character count, and reading time, so you can trim to a hard limit like "300 words max" without guessing. Many contest and university portals reject over-length entries automatically; checking before you submit beats rewriting after a rejection. Some limits are famously hard: IELTS Writing Task 2 requires at least 250 words.

Quick checklist:

  • Under the limit? Add one concrete example to a weak point.
  • Over the limit? Cut filler words first ("very," "really," "in order to").
  • Borderline? Aim 5% under the cap so last-minute edits don't push you over.

The four error patterns that never fully go away

Almost every L1 interference problem falls into one of four buckets — knowing the bucket makes the checker's flags learnable, not mysterious:

  • Articles (a/an/the). Most languages have no articles, so "the" is pure memorization. Fixes: "She works in the IT" → "She works in IT"; "I am student" → "I am a student."
  • Preposition collocations. English stores these pairs arbitrarily: you *make* a decision but *do* homework; you are *married to* someone, not "with"; a cost *depends on* the market, never "of." No rule predicts them — exposure does.
  • Register calibration. The sentence is grammatical but sounds wrong for the room — a text message written like a contract. More on this below.
  • Idiom translated literally (a calque). "I have cold" follows French and Spanish logic; English says "I'm cold." False friends: Spanish *actualmente* means "currently," not "actually," so "Actually, I live in Madrid" can reverse your meaning.

Sound natural, not robotic

Robotic text is the trap of writing to impress; natural text is writing to be understood, and it always lands better.

AI Humanizer

Soften stiff, translated, or AI-assisted text into phrasing a native reader expects, without losing your meaning.

How to use it: Run a stiff sentence through the AI Humanizer and compare the output to your original. Keep the version that sounds like you at your best — not the one that sounds like a textbook. Our guide on humanizing AI text covers full drafts.

Before / after:

  • Stiff: "I am writing to express my strong interest in the position of research assistant at your laboratory."
  • Natural: "I'd love to help out as a research assistant in your lab."

The meaning is the same; the second reads like a person who belongs there.

Paraphraser

Re-express a sentence you are unsure about in clearer English, then compare the versions.

How to use it: Paste the sentence you doubt into the Paraphraser. Glint offers several rewrites; pick the one whose wording you'd actually say aloud. Keep both versions side by side and read them aloud — the one that trips your tongue is usually the one to drop. For comparing without losing meaning, see our guide to paraphrasing without losing meaning.

When it helps most:

  • You translated a phrase literally and it "feels wrong" but you can't say why.
  • You repeated the same structure three sentences in a row.
  • You want a formal and a casual version to choose between.

Readability Analyzer

Check that your writing matches the reading level your reader expects, especially for public or academic text.

How to use it: Paste a passage into the Readability Analyzer for a grade-level and sentence-length readout. If the score is far above your reader (say, grade 14 for a blog), shorten sentences and swap long words. Aim one level below your reader's assumed level — clearer beats impressive when English is your second language. On the CEFR scale, most professional writing targets B2–C1 readers — roughly a grade-10-to-12 page.

Register: matching formality to the room

Professional conventions differ by culture, and this trips more writers than grammar does. English-language business email skews direct: state the request in the first two sentences — "Could you send the contract by Friday?" — because native readers read directness as efficient, not rude. If your first language builds requests through apologies and context, this will feel wrong to write and read right to your recipient. Americans even have a name for it: BLUF — bottom line up front.

Hedging is the other lever. Native professional English softens claims constantly: "This might affect the timeline" rather than "This will affect the timeline"; "may" and "might" buying politeness the way your first language does with courtesies. And small talk is not wasted: on an American or British call, thirty seconds of weather-and-weekend before business is the expected ramp. None of this lives in a grammar checker's rulebook — which is why the compare step matters more than the accept button.

A quick scenario

Imagine you must email a professor about a missed deadline. Your first draft: "Due to the occurrence of an unexpected personal circumstance, I was unable to submit the assignment at the previously established time." That's correct but stiff. Run it through the humanizer: "Something came up personally, so I couldn't turn in the assignment on time." Same facts, far warmer — and the professor is more likely to respond well.

Or picture a LinkedIn post. Stiff: "I am pleased to announce my completion of the project management certification." Natural: "Just finished my PM certification — excited to put it to work." Same news, twice the warmth.

Check your own work

Confidence comes from checking, not guessing — these two tools verify your writing the way a native speaker would.

How to use it: After you humanize and edit, paste the result into the AI Content Detector. A high "AI" score means smooth it again — real human writing has small unevenness a detector notices. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Pasting everything at once. Check one paragraph at a time so you learn the rule, not just the fix.
  • Accepting the first suggestion blindly. If a rewrite changes your meaning, keep your version.
  • Using the detector as a verdict. It's a tone hint, not proof — and measurably biased against non-native prose.
  • Skipping the readability step. Correct grammar can still be too dense for the reader.
  • Forgetting the humanizer on AI drafts. If you used AI to draft, humanize before you submit so it reads like you.

A simple daily habit

Draft in your own words first. Then grammar-check, humanize the awkward parts, and run a readability check. Over weeks, you will internalize the patterns the tools flag, and need them less. They are a coach, not a crutch.

The four-step loop: draft, check, compare, log

Make the loop explicit and it compounds:

  1. Draft in your own English first — never from the tool's output. The struggle to phrase a thought is where learning happens; skipping it means renting fluency forever.
  2. Run one mechanics pass — the grammar checker on one paragraph at a time.
  3. Compare instead of accepting. For every change, look at your original and ask what the difference is — the gap is the lesson.
  4. Keep a personal error log. A plain note file: wrong version, right version, rule — every time the checker flags something new. Review it weekly. Errors cluster by first language — Spanish speakers overuse articles, French speakers calque "actually" — and your log shows yours within a month.

The voice question

Polish the mechanics; keep the perspective. Readers connect with distinct viewpoints, not perfect grammar — an essay with a clear opinion and a slightly unusual phrase beats a flawless text anyone could have written. Your background shows in the details you notice — an asset, not a defect. An accent on the page works like an accent in speech: invisible when communication is clear, memorable when the thinking behind it is good. These tools exist to remove the friction that hides your thinking — not to sand off the thinking itself.
