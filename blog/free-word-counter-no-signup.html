Free Word Counter (No Signup): Count Words, Characters and More

Updated 2026-10-04 · 9 min read · by Glint AI Editorial Team

A word counter sounds like the simplest tool on the internet, and then a constraint depends on it. Essays have word limits. Social captions have character limits. Translators bill by the word. SEO posts need a rough length to look substantive. A free, no-signup word counter handles all of it in one box — no account, no upload, no fuss — and the difference between guessing and knowing is usually the difference between publishing and getting cut off mid-sentence.

Key takeaways

  • A word counter handles word and character limits for essays, captions, and SEO.
  • No-signup, browser-based counting keeps private drafts on your device.
  • Know whether your limit is words or characters before you write.
  • Watch average sentence length, not just totals.
  • Pair with the readability analyzer to catch both volume and density.

What a word counter actually counts

At minimum it counts words and characters. Useful versions also show sentences, paragraphs, and reading time, and let you separate characters-with-spaces from characters-without. That matters because a tweet limit is characters while an essay limit is words; mixing them up is a classic miss.

Some counters also estimate speaking time, which helps with scripts and presentations. The more signals you see, the better you can shape the text to its job.

Why the same text gets two different counts

Paste the same paragraph into two counters and you may get two answers, because "word" is not a legally defined unit. Is "state-of-the-art" one word or four? Does an em-dash split a sentence? Most tools tokenize on whitespace, so "state-of-the-art" counts as one and "can't" counts as one, but academic graders and publishers often use Microsoft Word's counter, which has its own edge-case rules. If a grade, a contract, or an invoice hangs on the number, count with the same tool the other party uses — not with whichever one is closest at hand.

Chinese, Japanese, and Korean break the whitespace assumption entirely. CJK text has no spaces between words, so a naive counter reads a 200-character Chinese paragraph as one "word." Serious counters detect CJK and count characters instead, which also matters on social platforms — see below, because platforms weight CJK characters differently too.

How "3 min read" gets calculated

Reading-time estimates are a word count plus an assumption. Silent reading averages roughly 200–250 words per minute for adults; reading aloud runs slower, around 150 wpm. Publishing platforms bake in their own numbers — Medium's documented read-time formula uses about 265 wpm and adds extra seconds per image, which is why image-heavy posts with the same word count can show different times. When a counter shows "3 min read," treat it as a negotiation point with the reader's attention, not a measurement of their eyes.

Why "no signup" matters here

You might paste a half-written draft, a private note, or client copy into a counter. A no-signup, browser-based counter processes the text on the page and never stores it. There is no account to create and no copy left on a server. For a tool this simple, that should be the default.

The privacy angle is not hypothetical. Freelancers paste unpublished client work. Students paste drafts of graded essays. Marketers paste embargoed campaign copy. Any tool that requires an account before counting has an incentive to keep what you paste, and a copy on a server is a copy you no longer control. Counting is arithmetic on text you already have; it deserves a tool that behaves like a calculator, not a filing cabinet.

Common uses

  • Essay and assignment limits. Students check they are at 487 words, not 520, before submitting. The Common App personal essay cuts off hard at 650 words — the form literally truncates, so 651 is not "close enough."
  • Social captions. Trim to the platform's character ceiling without guessing: X at 280, Instagram captions at 2,200, LinkedIn posts at 3,000. TikTok expanded captions from around 150 characters to 2,200 in 2021, and longer limits have rolled out to more accounts since.
  • SEO length targets. Hit a floor so a post reads as substantive. Meta titles truncate at roughly 50–60 characters (Google's display width is around 600 pixels), and meta descriptions at roughly 150–160 — beyond that, Google usually rewrites or cuts them.
  • Translation and freelance billing. Translation work is commonly quoted per word, so the count is literally the invoice. An estimate 8% high creates an awkward conversation; an exact count does not.
  • Ad copy. Google Ads responsive search ads allow headlines of 30 characters and descriptions of 90 — tight enough that counting is not optional.
  • Readability prep. Pair with a readability analyzer to catch both volume and density.

Keyword density checking

A counter that reports repeated-phrase frequency doubles as a density checker. Old-school SEO targeted roughly 1–2% keyword density; modern search engines lean on semantic matching, so the number itself matters far less than it did in 2010. The check is still worth running in one direction: if your target phrase appears 40 times in 1,000 words, you are keyword stuffing, and no semantic algorithm will rescue that. Density is a ceiling detector more than a target.

How to use it well

Set your target before you write, so you shape the draft instead of padding it after. Watch average sentence length, not just totals — a 180-word paragraph is a wall, and sentences averaging 30 words are exhausting. Count early and often; it is faster to stay in range than to cut later. If a section is bloated, the counter tells you which one to trim.

Cut or expand: making the decision

When a draft is over target, cut whole arguments, not syllables. Removing a weak paragraph improves the piece; shaving every sentence by 10% degrades all of them. When a draft is under target, add evidence — an example, a number, a counterargument — never adjectives. "Very important" adds two words and zero information; a worked example adds 150 words and all of it. The counter only tells you the gap size; this rule decides what fills it.

Threads, variants, and splitting work

Long-form-to-social is a counting problem. A 900-word post does not become a thread by magic: each X post gets 280 characters, and once you reserve space for numbering like "7/12," a reply handle, and a link, you are really writing to about 220. Professionals draft thread posts against a reduced budget precisely because they know the overhead is coming. The same discipline applies to A/B ad variants: write three headlines against the 30-character cap in one pass, and you can compare substance instead of discovering one variant is 34 characters and unusable.

Words versus characters

Learn the difference by platform, because they do not agree:

  • Words: essays, articles, most editorial and academic limits — the Common App at 650 words, for example.
  • Characters: X posts at 280, Instagram captions at 2,200, LinkedIn posts at 3,000, YouTube titles at 100.
  • Meta tags: titles at roughly 50–60 characters, descriptions at roughly 150–160.
  • SMS: 160 characters per message under the GSM-7 standard; concatenated messages fit 153 per segment because the reassembly header takes a slice.
  • Reading time: scripts and posts, derived from word count at roughly 200–250 wpm.

Knowing which unit your constraint uses prevents last-minute cuts that damage the writing.

Why platforms cap at all

Caps exist for the feed, not for you. Fixed or bounded lengths keep timelines scannable, force consistent rendering across devices, and raise the cost of spam. Meta limits exist because search results are a UI with finite pixels — the famous 50–60-character title limit is really a display-width limit, not a written rule anywhere in a spec. When a platform changes its cap, as X did moving from 140 to 280 in 2017, it is rebalancing feed density against expressiveness, not legislating quality.

The quirks insiders know

  • On X, every URL counts as 23 characters via t.co wrapping, regardless of its real length. A 10-character link counts 23; a 200-character link also counts 23. Your post can be under the limit until you add the link, then over.
  • X also weights CJK characters as 2 toward the 280, which is why the effective limit for Chinese, Japanese, and Korean text is roughly half — a deliberate nod to how much information each character carries.
  • On LinkedIn, only roughly the first 200 characters of a post show before the "see more" fold. The 3,000-character cap is real, but the first sentence does all the work.
  • Emoji break SMS into the UCS-2 encoding, dropping the per-message limit from 160 characters to 70. One emoji in a text campaign can halve your message and double your segments.

Free vs paid counters

Free counters cover counting, reading time, and basic stats. Paid tools add goal tracking, history, and team features — useful for an editorial operation running dozens of briefs a week, mostly unnecessary otherwise. For almost everyone, a free no-signup counter is enough; you are counting, not analyzing a corpus. Judge any paid counter by what it counts beyond the basics, because the basics are solved arithmetic and should never cost money.

Common mistakes

The main mistake is counting only at the end, then hacking the text to fit. Count as you go instead. Another is ignoring characters-with-spaces versus without; platform limits are almost always with-spaces. Do not treat word count as quality — a 2,000-word post can still be thin if every sentence is filler, and padding to hit a blog length target actively hurts: readers and search engines both measure engagement, and padded text fails both. Depth beats length, and the counter can only tell you which side of the limit you are on. Finally, remember that your count and the platform's count can disagree — X counts that 23-character URL whether you do or not.

Who should use it (and who shouldn't)

Use a word counter whenever a limit, a bill, or a readability goal depends on length. Skip the ritual if you are writing a personal note with no constraint; there, just write. But for any published or graded text, counting is the cheapest insurance against an embarrassing over- or under-shoot — and for billed work like translation, it is not insurance at all; it is the invoice.

A quick scenario: hit an essay limit

A student has a 1,500-word essay due. The draft comes in at 1,820 — over by 320. Rather than delete randomly, they paste it into the counter and watch the total as they cut.

First pass: remove a redundant paragraph (down to 1,640). Second: tighten introductions (1,560). Third: cut filler phrases like "in today's society" and "it is important to note" (1,505). The essay is now under the limit and, frankly, better than the longer version — which is the point. Cutting whole arguments and filler, per the rule above, upgraded the essay instead of merely shrinking it.

The counter turns editing from guesswork into a number you can manage. Without it, students either pad to hit a floor or panic-trim and lose their best lines. With it, they shape the text to the constraint on purpose.

Frequently asked questions

Is a no-signup word counter safe for private text? A browser-based counter processes text on the page and does not upload it, so private drafts stay on your device. No account means no stored copy — which matters when the text is client work, a graded essay, or embargoed campaign copy.

Does it count characters with or without spaces? Good counters show both. Use with-spaces for platform limits like X's 280 characters and Instagram's 2,200-character captions, and without-spaces when a style guide or submission form specifies it.

Can a word counter check reading time? Many do, estimating minutes from word count at roughly 200–250 words per minute for silent reading. Treat it as a guide — platforms like Medium use their own formulas (around 265 wpm plus time per image), and actual speed varies by reader and content.

Why not just use my editor's built-in count? You can, but a standalone counter works on any text anywhere, including snippets you do not want to paste into a document — and counters disagree on edge cases like hyphenated words, so use the tool your grader or client uses when the number is binding.
