What Reading Ease Score Your Landing Page Actually Needs

Updated Aug 6, 2026 · 9 min read · by Glint AI Editorial Team

Most landing pages are written for the founder, not the visitor. The result: copy that scores like a corporate legal memo and converts like one too. Here's the number you should actually aim for — and how to hit it.

Key takeaways

  • Most landing pages are written for the founder, not the visitor, and score like legal memos.
  • Benchmark by page type: hero about 70 to 80, feature blurbs about 60 to 70, legal fine print can be lower.
  • The mistakes that tank your score: long sentences, jargon, passive voice, dense paragraphs.
  • Reading ease indirectly helps SEO by improving engagement and time on page.
  • Raise the score without a rewrite: shorten the worst five sentences, cut filler, use active voice.

What "reading ease" means

The most common metric is the Flesch Reading Ease score. It runs from 0 (very hard) to 100 (very easy), based on two things: average words per sentence, and average syllables per word. Short sentences and plain words score high.

Score = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words / sentences)
        − 84.6 × (syllables / words)

Don't chase 100. A score of 90+ often reads like it's written for a child. For a SaaS or B2B site, 60–75 is the sweet spot: clear, but still credible.

Reading ease by industry, not just page type

The page-type table above is a starting point, but the right score also depends on who you sell to. A consumer fitness app can sit at 75–85 because the reader is half-distracted on a phone. A developer-tool landing page can legitimately sit at 50–60 because the audience expects precise, technical language and will distrust copy that's too simple. Match the score to the reader's expertise and context, not to a universal ideal.

The mistakes that tank your score

Most low scores come from a handful of repeat offenders:

  • Nominalizations. "We performed an analysis of" → "We analyzed". Verbs are shorter and clearer than noun forms.
  • Stacked modifiers. "Highly optimized, fully automated, enterprise-ready solution" — three adjectives where one would do.
  • Hidden verbs. "Provide assistance" → "help"; "make a decision" → "decide".
  • Long lead-ins. "It is important to note that" — just delete it and start with the point.

A reusable readability checklist

Run this before any page goes live:

  1. Average sentence length under 17 words.
  2. One idea per sentence.
  3. No word longer than three syllables where a shorter one exists.
  4. Every paragraph answers "so what?" for the reader.
  5. Score the body copy, not the nav or buttons.

How to A/B test copy readability

Readability is testable, not just measurable. In your A/B tool, keep the offer and design identical and swap only the copy: variant A at ~55, variant B at ~72. Watch bounce rate and time-on-page for two weeks. Often the easier version wins not because it's smarter, but because it lowers the cost of the first scroll. Let the score follow the conversion data, not the other way around.

Reading ease and SEO: the indirect link

Google does not have a "reading ease" ranking signal you can point to. But the behaviors readable pages earn — people stay, scroll, and do not bounce — are exactly the engagement signals Google does watch. Clean, well-structured copy also tends to earn featured snippets because it states answers plainly. Treat readability as a conversion and retention lever first; the SEO benefit is the compounding side effect.

Why it moves conversions

Reading ease isn't vanity. Every extra cognitive step between the visitor and the "why should I care" answer is a chance for them to leave. Shorter sentences lower the cost of understanding. You're not dumbing down — you're removing friction.

How to raise your score (without rewriting everything)

  • Split run-on sentences. If a sentence has two commas and a semicolon, it's probably two sentences.
  • Swap $5 words for $1 words. "Utilize" → "use", "facilitate" → "help", "leverage" → "use".
  • Lead with the verb. "Our platform enables teams to ship faster" → "Ship faster with our platform."
  • Cut the hedges. "In order to" → "to"; "at this point in time" → "now".

Quick wins you can ship this week

You don't need a full rewrite to move the number. Three fast changes: (1) cut your hero subhead to one sentence under 14 words; (2) replace every "We help you to" with a direct verb; (3) split any paragraph longer than four sentences. These alone typically lift a homepage from the 40s into the 60s, and they take an afternoon. Measure before and after with the analyzer so you can show the change to a client or co-founder with proof, not opinion.

Worked example

Before (score ~28): "Our comprehensive, enterprise-grade platform facilitates the seamless orchestration of cross-functional workflows."

After (score ~75): "Our platform helps your teams work together without the chaos."

Same claim. Half the syllables. Twice the clarity.

FAQ

Is a high score always better? No. Technical and legal pages should score lower because the audience expects precision. Match the score to the reader.

Does Google rank by reading ease? Not directly, but readable pages have lower bounce rates and longer dwell time — both correlation signals Google uses.

Can I check a whole page at once? Paste the main copy block. Headings and nav don't count toward comprehension the same way body text does.
