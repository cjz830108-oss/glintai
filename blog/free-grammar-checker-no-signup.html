What a good free grammar checker catches

A real checker does more than paint red squiggles under typos. Here is what a good one surfaces, and what each bucket means for your writing. Most people only need about three of these buckets on any given day, and a good tool tells you which ones fired.

  • Grammar: subject-verb agreement, pronoun case, tense slips, and fragments with no main clause.
  • Spelling: typos like recieve and seperate, repeated words, and confused terms a basic spellcheck misses.
  • Punctuation: missing commas, comma splices, apostrophe errors, and run-ons that need a period or semicolon.
  • Clarity: sentences that are correct but hard to follow, usually because they run long or double up.
  • Style: wordy phrasing, passive voice, and repeated vocabulary that flattens your prose.

None of this replaces your judgment. It catches the errors your eye skips on the tenth read of the same paragraph. Common mistakes it fixes

A few error types show up in nearly every draft, no matter how careful the writer:

  • Subject-verb agreement: the list of items are long should read is long, because the subject list is singular.
  • Comma splices: two full sentences joined by a comma. Split with a period or join with a semicolon.
  • Homophones: their, there, and they're sound alike but mean different things.
  • Tense shifts: she walks in and sat down mixes present and past. Pick one and stay in it.

Catching these by hand takes a focus most people lack at the end of a long day. The checker never tires, which is why a no-sign-up tool belongs in your daily routine. Another frequent catch is its versus it's: it's is a contraction of it is, while its shows possession. Mixing them is among the most common slips in professional writing, and a quick pass removes it before anyone notices.

Articles cause quiet trouble too. Writers say a when they mean an before a vowel sound, or drop the from a phrase that needs it. None of this is dramatic, but together it makes writing feel off. A checker that explains the rule, not just the fix, teaches you to stop making the mistake in the first place. That is the real win. Teachers see it constantly, and so do editors who inherit someone else's draft.

Tips to level up your writing

A tool catches errors, but it does not build taste. To improve faster than the red lines alone can teach you:

  • Read your work aloud. Awkward phrasing becomes obvious the moment you hear it.
  • Cut filler words. Just, really, very, and actually rarely earn their place.
  • Vary sentence length so paragraphs breathe instead of marching in lockstep.
  • Keep a personal error list of the mistakes you make most, and scan for those first.

A 30-second pre-send checklist

Before any important message leaves your hands, run this fast pass with the checker open:

  1. Scan the first sentence. If it buries the point, lead with it instead.
  2. Check every name, date, and number for a typo the checker might miss on its own.
  3. Accept grammar and spelling fixes, but skip style changes that alter your meaning.
  4. Read the final line aloud. If it sounds wrong, it probably is.

None of this takes more than half a minute, and it catches the errors that cost the most when they slip through.
