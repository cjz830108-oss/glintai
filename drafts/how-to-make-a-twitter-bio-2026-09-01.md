# How to Write a Twitter/X Bio That Gets the Right Followers

Your Twitter/X bio is the first thing a stranger checks before deciding to follow, and it has to close the deal in 160 characters. It is not your resume and not a tagline — it is a tiny ad for who you are and why someone should care. A good bio attracts the right people and filters the wrong ones. Written free and with no signup, it is a two-minute upgrade that pays back every time your profile is visited. This guide covers the real constraints, the anatomy that works, before-and-after rewrites, and how to keep the line honest as you grow.

## What a Twitter/X bio does

It tells visitors who you are, what you talk about, and what they get by following. In 160 characters it sets expectations. A clear bio earns the right follow; a vague one earns none or the wrong ones. It is also the line people screenshot when they introduce you, so it doubles as your one-line identity.

### The three places your bio actually appears

Most people think of the bio as profile-page furniture. It actually surfaces in three places, two of which you never see:

- **Your profile page**, under the name, where visitors decide to follow or leave.
- **Search results and hover cards**: X indexes bio text, so a search that lands on the People tab can surface you by a word in your bio — and hovering your @handle anywhere shows a card with the bio on it.
- **Screenshot introductions**: when someone recommends you in a thread, the shorthand they paste is usually your bio line.

That search behavior is the detail most guides miss: people type things like "newsletter growth" into X search and read the bios that come back. Write with those searchers in mind and the bio works twice.

### The name field is a search slot, not a label

X gives you two text fields people mistake for one job. The @handle is permanent-ish and not searchable. The name field — up to 50 characters, displayed in bold above the handle — is what X matches against search queries. Adding one honest descriptor there, the way journalists put their beat ("Maya Chen · Climate Policy") or founders put the category ("Sam Ruiz · Hiring Software"), is not keyword stuffing. It is how discovery on the platform works. The limit is taste: one descriptor reads as positioning; a pipe-separated wall of five reads as spam and gets you skipped even when you rank.

## When to rewrite your bio

- **A new niche** where your old bio no longer fits.
- **A follower plateau** that hints your pitch is unclear.
- **A rebrand** or career change.
- **Before a launch** when profile traffic spikes.
- **After a viral post** that sends strangers to your profile.
- **Quarterly, on a calendar**. Bios go stale the way resume objectives do: positioning drifts, the work changes, and the line keeps describing who you were last spring.

The viral-post case is the urgent one: a post that travels sends thousands of profile visits in a day — strangers deciding in seconds. If the bio still describes the project you quit in March, that traffic evaporates without a trace. This is the wrong place to save time.

## How to write a good bio

The anatomy that consistently works fits five things into 160 characters:

- **Who you help** — "for DTC brands", "helping PhD students finish".
- **What you do** — in plain words a stranger understands on one read.
- **Proof** — a number, a client, a milestone. "Ex-Stripe", "12k subscribers", "3 products shipped" all count; borrowed credibility counts if it is true.
- **One CTA** — the follow promise ("weekly teardowns") or an action ("free template ↓").
- **One link** — direct or link-in-bio, chosen on purpose (tradeoffs below).

Skip clichés like "passionate about" and "thought leader." Use the few characters for specifics, not adjectives. Because Glint's helpers run in your browser, you can draft bios without uploading your profile anywhere.

### First person beats third person (if you are the product)

"I help e-commerce brands write emails that get opened" reads as a human. "Maya is a marketing expert who helps brands" reads as an assistant wrote it. Third person is correct for companies, publications, and public figures with comms teams. If you are a solo operator, first person is the honest register — you are making a promise to the reader, not writing your own encyclopedia entry.

### Emoji as bullets, not decoration

The emoji that earns its place is the one doing layout work: a line break's more visible cousin. Arrows (↓) pointing at the link, a divider between the pitch and the CTA — that is functional. Decoration emoji — the rocket, the sparkle, the coffee cup — cost characters and say nothing. The test: if you delete the emoji and the line still scans, it was decoration.

### Line breaks for scannability

X preserves line breaks in bios, and the mobile app's edit screen is the most reliable place to set them — some third-party clients strip them on save, so check your profile after editing from anywhere unusual. Three short lines beat one dense paragraph because profile visitors scan rather than read:

- Line 1: what you do and who for
- Line 2: proof
- Line 3: the promise and the link

### Pair it with a pinned post that proves it

The bio makes a promise; the pinned post is the evidence. If the bio says "weekly teardowns," the pinned post should be the latest teardown. If it says "build logs," pin the build log. A visitor who reads a promise and sees proof one scroll away follows at a different rate than one who finds a pinned meme from 2024. Treat the bio and the pin as a unit, and refresh both together.

## A step-by-step method

1. **Name your role** in plain words a stranger understands.
2. **Say who it helps** or what topic you own.
3. **Add one proof** — a result, a number, a project. An invented number is worse than no number.
4. **Give a reason to follow** — a promise or a rhythm ("new drop every Friday").
5. **Choose the link** — one destination, picked for one job.
6. **Trim to fit** under 160 characters without losing the hook.

Then read it aloud. If a sentence sounds like a LinkedIn headline, it is a cliché wearing a costume.

## A worked example

- Identity: "Just a guy" (weak) → "Indie maker building tiny tools" (strong)
- Value: "I tweet stuff" (weak) → "Sharing what I ship & what I learn" (strong)
- Hook: none (weak) → "New drop every Friday" (strong)

The strong version tells a stranger exactly what they get and when, which is what earns the follow.

### Before and after: three rewrites

**Before:** "Digital marketer. Coffee lover. Dog mom." Three noun phrases, zero audience, zero proof, nothing to follow. **After:** "I write email flows for DTC skincare brands. $2M in attributed client revenue last year. Free teardown every Tuesday ↓." Specific audience, verifiable proof, a rhythm worth subscribing to. Swap in your own true number — the structure only works when the proof is real.

**Before:** "Aspiring entrepreneur. Hustling every day." "Aspiring" is the tell: it promises nothing that exists. **After:** "Building a meal-planning app in public. 400 users, week 9. Follow for build logs and the occasional disaster." The rewrite trades aspiration for a live project and a status line people can check next month.

**Before:** "Thought leader | Speaker | Innovator | Disruptor." Every title deleted by the rewrite: **"I teach Excel to accountants. 20k students through my courses. Free shortcuts sheet below."** Every remaining claim points at something a visitor can verify, which is why the rewrite survives skepticism.

## Bio styles compared

- **Builder** — best when you ship in public. Hook example: "Shipping in public, weekly." It fails when the shipping stalls: the bio becomes a museum of a dead project, and regular followers notice first.
- **Educator** — best for a teaching audience. Hook: "Practical tips, no fluff." It fails when the topic is too broad — "marketing tips" is a category, not a position.
- **Personality** — best for personal brands built on takes. Hook: "Honest takes on X." It fails fastest of the three: if the takes stop, the bio is an overpromise.

Pick one and let the other two live in your posts. A bio that tries to be all three is a bio nobody can follow for any specific reason.

### The one link: direct vs link-in-bio

- **Direct link** sends every click to one page — best when you have a single conversion goal like a newsletter signup or a product. It never adds a click between the visitor and the destination.
- **A link-in-bio page** (Linktree and similar) hosts several destinations — best when you juggle multiple projects or rotate campaigns. The cost is one extra click plus a second page you now have to maintain.

Rule of thumb: one thing to promote, link direct; three or more, use a hub page and prune it quarterly along with the bio.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague identity** — "just a guy who likes tech" tells nobody anything.
- **Stuffing the name field** — one descriptor is positioning; a wall of keywords reads as spam in both search and the follow decision.
- **Hashtags in the bio** — hashtags do their work in posts; your bio is already indexed by its plain words.
- **Never updating it** — the bio drifts from what you actually post and confuses new visitors.
- **A dead or lazy link** — a link to a homepage nobody has touched in a year converts worse than no link.
- **Pasting your profile into a web generator** — leaks data you did not need to share.

## Who should use it (and who shouldn't)

Write a clear bio if you post publicly and want followers. Skip overthinking if your account is private and personal — there, a line to friends is enough. For any public presence, the bio pays for itself.
