# Free AI Tools for Nonprofits: Grant Copy, Donor Emails, and Impact Reports

Most small nonprofits do not have a communications department. They have one executive director who writes the grant application on Sunday, the donor newsletter on Monday, and the board deck on Thursday, usually with a volunteer helping where they can. This guide covers how to cut that workload with free, browser-based AI tools that need no account, no credit card, and no per-seat license, along with the checks that keep an AI-assisted grant application honest.

## What a $0 comms stack has to do

The constraint at a small charity is rarely willingness to pay. It is that the buying process costs more than the tool. A per-seat subscription looks cheap until you count who needs it: two staff, four board members, and a rotating cast of comms volunteers. At twenty dollars a seat, a dozen occasional users is nearly three thousand dollars a year, plus the work of adding accounts and removing them when volunteers move on.

That overhead is why no-signup browser tools fit this sector: nothing to provision, nothing to offboard, no shared password on a sticky note.

The work itself is narrower than most software assumes. A letter of inquiry, a grant narrative, an appeal, thank-you notes, a newsletter, an impact report, a one-page board summary, and recruitment posts. Nearly all the effort goes into compression, consistency, and proofreading.

## Grant narratives: the word limit is the real first draft

Ask anyone who writes small-foundation applications where the time goes and they will not say research. They will say cutting. Narrative fields commonly land between 500 and 1,500 words, and an honest first answer to "describe the need" is usually twice the limit.

The mistake is counting while writing. You end up trimming sentences you have not finished thinking through. Write the full answer first, then compress in a deliberate order.

Paste the draft into a [word counter](/tools/word-counter.html) to see your real position. Counters disagree slightly on hyphenated words and headings, so if the funder's portal shows its own count, trust that one.

Cut in this order: repeated need statements the funder already read in your letter of inquiry, then hedging and adverbs, then background the form collects elsewhere, then sentences over thirty words. Leave the numbers alone on every pass.

Before: "Our organization has been serving the community for over a decade, and during that time we have consistently worked to provide critical support services to families experiencing housing instability across the three-county region we serve." That is 38 words.

After: "For eleven years we have housed 412 families across three counties." Eleven words, and it says something the first version did not.

### Reusing a narrative without copying it

Recycling your own language across funders is sensible. Pasting the same paragraph into every application is not, because the questions differ and reviewers notice boilerplate that does not answer what was asked. A [paraphraser](/tools/paraphraser.html) is useful for the organizational background section, which rarely changes. Rewrite it for the specific question, then read the result against your own facts.

Stated plainly: funders increasingly ask how applicants used AI. Answer honestly, and never submit a figure or beneficiary story you have not checked against your records.

## Donor emails: one ask, one number, one check

Nonprofit email benchmarks are usually quoted in a broad band, roughly 25 to 40 percent open rates depending on list size. Treat that as context, not a target you can hit with phrasing. What moves a small list is more boring: a real person as the sender, a subject line that fits on a phone, and one clear ask.

Keep subject lines in the 40 to 60 character range and front-load the concrete part, because most inboxes truncate after that. Avoid all caps and exclamation marks, and do not make the subject the ask. "Please Support Our Annual Fall Appeal" gives nothing back. "Your $40 fed the Alvarez family for a week" gives an outcome before the email is opened.

The body follows a short shape. Line one states what already happened because of them. The middle makes one specific ask tied to one specific unit: $40 covers a week of groceries for a family of four. The close has one link, not four, since competing links split the click and reduce both.

Run the finished text through a [grammar checker](/tools/grammar-checker.html), then read it out loud. Grammar tools catch surface errors, not a sentence that is clean and emotionally wrong for someone being asked for money. ### Thank-you notes at volume

Thank-you notes are where small teams lose the most ground, because they arrive all at once after an appeal. Draft one strong note, then vary the opening line and outcome for each segment: first-time, recurring, and in-kind donors. A [paraphraser](/tools/paraphraser.html) generates those variations quickly, but the donor's name, the amount, and the designated fund must come from your own records.

## Impact reports and board summaries

The distinction that makes an impact report useful is outputs versus outcomes. An output is 1,200 meals served. An outcome is that 84 percent of responding families reported no food insecurity ninety days later. Board members and funders want both.

A one-page board summary works in three blocks: what happened this period, what it cost, and what we need. Add a red, amber, or green marker to each program so the meeting starts in the right place.

Use an [AI text summarizer](/tools/ai-text-summarizer.html) to reduce each program's notes to four or five lines, then read the condensed versions side by side to spot cross-program patterns, which are much harder to see when each program's notes run four pages. Assemble the summary, hold it to one page with a [word counter](/tools/word-counter.html), then proofread.

One warning matters more here than anywhere else: a summarizer will delete the qualifier that makes a number true. "84 percent of the 62 families who responded" can come back as "84 percent of families," a stronger claim than your data supports. After any summarizing pass, re-attach every denominator by hand.

## A quick scenario

A small food pantry with two paid staff, about thirty active volunteers, and an annual budget near $240,000 has three deadlines in one weekend: a 750-word grant narrative due Tuesday, a 400-word year-end appeal, and a one-page board summary for Thursday.

Saturday morning goes to the narrative. The ED writes the long answer first, gets 1,480 words, and cuts to 743 using the order above, keeping every figure. The background section is rewritten from last year's successful application with a [paraphraser](/tools/paraphraser.html) so it answers this funder's actual question.

Saturday afternoon is the appeal. She replaces the subject line with the concrete outcome line, runs the copy through a [grammar checker](/tools/grammar-checker.html), and checks the single number in it against the finance report, because that is the number a donor remembers.

Sunday morning is the board packet. Six program leads sent two to four pages each. An [AI text summarizer](/tools/ai-text-summarizer.html) reduces them, and a pattern jumps out: three programs are reporting the same transportation problem, which becomes the first agenda item. The summary is held to a page with a [word counter](/tools/word-counter.html).

Sunday afternoon goes to the volunteer recruitment post, with tags from a [hashtag generator](/tools/hashtag-generator.html). Total time on mechanical work across the weekend is about four hours instead of a day and a half.

## Volunteer recruitment and social posts

Recruitment is a volume game on a small budget, and the posts that work share a shape. Name the shift and time, say what the person will actually do, add one line of impact, and give one link. Posts that open with the mission rather than the task tend to underperform, because a potential volunteer is deciding whether the shift fits into their Saturday.

Three to five hashtags is a reasonable range: one for the cause, one or two for the locality, and one tied to the campaign. A [hashtag generator](/tools/hashtag-generator.html) is faster than guessing, and the reasoning is in the [hashtag generator guide](/blog/hashtag-generator-guide.html).

## Where free tools stop

Browser tools do not connect to your donor CRM, remember your voice across documents, schedule posts, or do multi-source research. If the task is reconciling donor records or producing audited financials, spend the money on proper software.

They also do not know your programs. Every statistic and date has to come from your own data, and the tool's job is only to help you shape it. The same $0 logic applies to the rest of the organization's administrative writing, covered in [AI tools for small business](/blog/ai-tools-for-small-business-2026.html).

## Common mistakes

- Letting a compression pass delete a figure, date, or sample size from a grant narrative.
- Submitting a statistic the tool produced without checking it against your program records.
- Copying last year's narrative verbatim instead of answering the question that was asked.
- Sending an appeal with four links, which splits the click and lowers the response.
- Letting a summarizer strip the qualifier that makes a percentage honest.
- Writing the narrative while watching the word counter, which produces thin sentences.
- Paying per seat for occasional volunteer users when the task needs no account.
