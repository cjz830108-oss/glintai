## What Screenwriters Actually Need from AI (Research, Coverage, Format — Not Ghostwriting)

Screenwriting is a craft with industry-standard formats, and that changes what AI is useful for. A novelist can draft 60,000 words and revise at leisure. A screenwriter's output is judged by producers, contest readers, and coverage services against conventions — page counts, synopsis lengths, logline economy — that are unforgiving and easy to verify. The valuable AI work is therefore mechanical and research-shaped:

- Condensing source material and research into usable briefs
- Trimming loglines, synopses, and treatments to accepted lengths
- Proofing dialogue and action lines for typos and drift
- Rewriting pitch documents for different audiences without losing your voice
- Checking whether a writing sample reads as machine-generated before you submit it
- Cleaning up visuals for pitch decks

What it is not useful for: ghostwriting your screenplay. Coverage readers and executives have become fluent in detecting unedited AI prose, and a script that reads generated is dead on arrival. Use the tools on everything around the script, and the script stays yours.

### The Format Gate: Courier 12pt, Sluglines, and the One-Page Rule

Screenwriting has a physical format, and every gatekeeper — contest reader, coverage service, producer's assistant — checks it before absorbing a word of story. The standard is Courier 12pt, and it is not nostalgia: the monospaced font is what makes the industry's timing convention work, one properly formatted page equaling roughly one minute of screen time. A 110-page spec is sold as a two-hour movie because the page count says so. The elements themselves are a grammar worth knowing cold: sluglines (scene headings) open every scene with INT. or EXT., the location, and the time of day — INT. DINER — NIGHT; action lines run in present tense and describe only what can be seen or heard; character cues go in caps above dialogue; parentheticals stay sparse; and the extensions do precise work — (V.O.) marks voice-over laid over the picture, while (O.S.) marks a character who is in the scene but off screen. One slugline nuance that marks a professional: when a scene follows immediately in the same location or picks up the action mid-flow, the time reads CONTINUOUS rather than DAY or NIGHT — readers notice who controls the clock.

The format also encodes the script's stage of life, and confusing the stages is a rookie tell. A spec script — written on speculation, hoping to sell — contains no scene numbers, no camera angles, and no "CUT TO:" padding; adding them tells every reader you do not know the difference between a spec and a shooting script. A shooting script exists only after a sale or production greenlight, with numbered scenes, revision marks (asterisks on changed lines, then colored pages per revision set), and production elements added. You write the first one; you almost never need to fake the second.

### The WGA and AI Reality

Without wading into contract specifics, the direction is clear: writers' labor agreements now treat AI-generated material as something that cannot replace a writer's credit or compensation, and studios are cautious about ingesting unsolicited scripts into third-party models. The 2023 WGA agreement made the baseline explicit in broad terms: AI-generated material cannot be used to undermine a writer's credit or compensation, a writer may choose to use AI but the company cannot require it, and AI output does not count as source material in the credit calculus. For a spec writer, the practical takeaway is simpler than any clause — never paste your screenplay into a cloud AI you cannot audit. Browser-local tools sidestep the entire question. Several studios followed with policies of their own requiring disclosure of AI use and restricting what may be uploaded to third-party services; treat any cloud tool's retention terms as unknown until you have read them yourself.

## Screenplay-Specific Workflows: Coverage Reports, Treatments, Pitch Docs

Three workflows where the stack comes together.

The coverage response loop. You receive six pages of coverage on your last draft. Paste it into the summarizer and extract the pattern: three notes mention a sagging middle, two mention a passive protagonist. Fixing the pattern beats fixing each note. Then rewrite your one-page synopsis against the revised draft and verify the length with the counter before resubmitting.

The treatment assembly loop. A treatment pulls from everywhere — research briefs from the summarizer, a pitch paragraph reworked through the paraphraser, loglines trimmed with the counter. Assemble section by section, and keep every source document local; browser-only tools mean your treatment never exists on a server you did not choose.

The submission-prep loop. Before a contest or fellowship: grammar pass on the first ten pages, detector pass on the sample, background-remover pass on any deck visuals. Thirty minutes of mechanical checking protects months of writing.

### The Spec Market: Managers, Contests, and the First Ten Pages

The market reality worth internalizing early: spec scripts are bought through relationships. Managers and agents are the gate — most producers and studios will not open an unsolicited script, and "querying" means emailing a query letter asking for representation, not attaching the script. Contests and fellowships are the sanctioned workaround, because a placement forces a stranger to read your pages: the Academy Nicholl Fellowship and the Austin Film Festival screenwriting competition are the two most credible lines on a query letter, and even a Nicholl quarterfinal placement or an Austin second-round score is a signal managers act on. The query letter itself is short and judged fast: a logline, one sentence of personal credibility — a contest placement, a produced short, a writers' assistant credit — and a polite ask. A manager reads it in under a minute and is really asking one question: does this logline sound like a movie? Inside any evaluation, the first ten pages carry most of the weight — readers form their judgment there, and coverage, the two-to-four-page internal report graded PASS, CONSIDER, or RECOMMEND, flows from it. That is exactly why the submission-prep loop above concentrates its mechanical checks on the sample's opening rather than spreading them evenly across 110 pages.

## What AI Can't Do (and Why You Still Own the Story)

Be honest about the boundary. AI cannot structure a screenplay — it can imitate structure, but it does not know what your second act is actually about. It cannot write subtext, because subtext requires knowing what characters hide, which requires caring. It cannot take a note, which is 80 percent of professional screenwriting: translating "the middle sags" into a specific, surgical rewrite is a human skill.

What AI can do is remove the friction around the craft. Research becomes briefs instead of weekend archives. Loglines get to 40 words instead of 68. Coverage patterns surface in minutes. A formatting-compliance pass and a dialogue pass that flags on-the-nose lines are genuinely good mechanical help; original structure and subtext are not, and no model change in 2026 altered that boundary. The stack handles the mechanical layer so your hours go to the only thing that sells a script — the story only you can tell.
