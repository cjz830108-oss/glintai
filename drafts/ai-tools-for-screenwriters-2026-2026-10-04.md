## What Screenwriters Actually Need from AI (Research, Coverage, Format — Not Ghostwriting)

Screenwriting is a craft with industry-standard formats, and that changes what AI is useful for. A novelist can draft 60,000 words and revise at leisure. A screenwriter's output is judged by producers, contest readers, and coverage services against conventions — page counts, synopsis lengths, logline economy — that are unforgiving and easy to verify. The valuable AI work is therefore mechanical and research-shaped:

- Condensing source material and research into usable briefs
- Trimming loglines, synopses, and treatments to accepted lengths
- Proofing dialogue and action lines for typos and drift
- Rewriting pitch documents for different audiences without losing your voice
- Checking whether a writing sample reads as machine-generated before you submit it
- Cleaning up visuals for pitch decks

What it is not useful for: ghostwriting your screenplay. Coverage readers and executives have become fluent in detecting unedited AI prose, and a script that reads generated is dead on arrival. Use the tools on everything around the script, and the script stays yours.

### The WGA and AI Reality

Without wading into contract specifics, the direction is clear: writers' labor agreements now treat AI-generated material as something that cannot replace a writer's credit or compensation, and studios are cautious about ingesting unsolicited scripts into third-party models. For a spec writer, the practical takeaway is simpler than any clause — never paste your screenplay into a cloud AI you cannot audit. Browser-local tools sidestep the entire question.

### Trim Loglines and Synopses to Industry Length (Word Counter)

Industry lengths are unforgiving: a logline should land around 25 to 50 words, a synopsis on one page (roughly 400 to 500 words), a treatment at two to five pages. Paste your draft into the [free word counter](/tools/word-counter.html) and cut in this order: character names a stranger would not recognize, backstory, and any sentence that describes theme instead of events. The counter runs in the browser with no signup, so it is the fastest way to check a logline before it goes in an email subject line.

### Rewrite Pitches Without Losing Your Voice (Paraphraser)

Verify every claim after the rewrite — paraphrasing tools preserve meaning statistically, not journalistically — and never let it touch dialogue, where voice is the product.

## Screenplay-Specific Workflows: Coverage Reports, Treatments, Pitch Docs

Three workflows where the stack comes together.

The coverage response loop. You receive six pages of coverage on your last draft. Paste it into the summarizer and extract the pattern: three notes mention a sagging middle, two mention a passive protagonist. Fixing the pattern beats fixing each note. Then rewrite your one-page synopsis against the revised draft and verify the length with the counter before resubmitting.

The treatment assembly loop. A treatment pulls from everywhere — research briefs from the summarizer, a pitch paragraph reworked through the paraphraser, loglines trimmed with the counter. Assemble section by section, and keep every source document local; browser-only tools mean your treatment never exists on a server you did not choose.

The submission-prep loop. Before a contest or fellowship: grammar pass on the first ten pages, detector pass on the sample, background-remover pass on any deck visuals. Thirty minutes of mechanical checking protects months of writing.

## What AI Can't Do (and Why You Still Own the Story)

Be honest about the boundary. AI cannot structure a screenplay — it can imitate structure, but it does not know what your second act is actually about. It cannot write subtext, because subtext requires knowing what characters hide, which requires caring. It cannot take a note, which is 80 percent of professional screenwriting: translating "the middle sags" into a specific, surgical rewrite is a human skill.

What AI can do is remove the friction around the craft. Research becomes briefs instead of weekend archives. Loglines get to 40 words instead of 68. Coverage patterns surface in minutes. The stack handles the mechanical layer so your hours go to the only thing that sells a script — the story only you can tell.
